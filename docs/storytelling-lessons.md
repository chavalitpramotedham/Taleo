# Storytelling lessons

What the storyteller has been taught, and why - from tests on whole books
written through the real storyteller, with the same setups and choices,
judged blind against the previous version (clarity, coherence, appeal, and
every confusing moment listed).

## 1. Clarity comes first (15 Nov 2026)

Books for the youngest were confusing to read aloud: a "dino stone" nobody
had met, a star that suddenly talked, a friend left stuck in the mud and
then fine, a lost duck turning up held by a toy no one had mentioned, three
threads at once (a fallen tower, a dino hunt, a lost duck).

The cause was the instructions themselves. Every page was asked for a
giggle, a hook to the next page, a never-repeated new word to teach, a
fresh visible action, a refrain and a surprise - and nothing asked for one
problem told in order. On a page of 16 words the demands won and the story
lost: actions were picked to fit the page's word, surprises were random.

What changed:

- **Clear and sensible is now the first priority**, above engagement and
  the moral: one want, shown at the start; every page following from the
  last, with causes the child can see; introduced before used, and always
  called the same thing; characters who act and speak sensibly (never
  amused when a friend is upset); objects that only talk if the story made
  them magical; threads kept (stuck friends helped out, promises of help
  kept, teases paid on the next page); a simple map (characters get to new
  places on a page); humour that makes sense the moment it happens.
- **The youngest (under 3)** get their own rules: one goal, at most one new
  character and one new thing a segment, each page one small step said
  plainly, the same word for the same thing, central characters who do
  things.
- **The plot problem is the test of the lesson.** In a sharing story the
  problem is whether to share - an accident must never make the choice
  for the characters. (Dropping this line in an earlier draft let a gust of
  wind solve the sharing story, and older books got worse.)
- **A private plan**, written with segment 1 and handed back with every
  later one: want, problem, ending - three sentences. Planning the steps in
  between made older books contort themselves back onto the plan after each
  choice; the destination alone keeps them on one line.
- **Gentle themes get gentle wants** (say goodnight to the moon), not an
  invented mishap; nothing starts out lost or broken unless we see it
  happen.
- **Choices name only what the child has met**, and the next segment
  follows from whichever is chosen.
- **A typed idea is answered once.** The aside is read out before the page,
  so the page must not answer it again (this needed saying in the schema as
  well as the prompt - "show the consequence of the choice" was pulling the
  other way). An idea that fits is woven in as a way to tackle the one
  problem; one that doesn't is answered by the aside alone, never forced in.
- The word to teach is taken from what the page needs to happen anyway;
  a central character written out again as "new" is folded back into the
  one we have.

Results (clarity out of 10, old -> new, averaged over judgings): ages 1-2
lost item 5 -> 8, ages 2-3 big feeling 4 -> 8, ages 2-3 party 5 -> 6, ages
3-4 adventure 7 -> 7, ages 1-2 bedtime 8 -> 7.5, ages 4-5 mystery 7.5 -> 6,
ages 6+ sharing 6 -> 8. Mean 6.1 -> 7.2; the gains are largest at the ages
where the problem was.

Still to look at: mysteries (ages 4-5) - clue logic and repeated
confessions. Single books vary a lot, and so does the judge (one book
scored 5, 7 and 8 on three readings): compare several books per setup
before trusting a small difference.

Cost: about $0.10 a book with the storyteller, $0.01 a judgement.
